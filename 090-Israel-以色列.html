历史参引与要点 / Historical References; Points of Interest

ENISRAEL: the Hebrew nation, or Jewish people. As a nation or people, including all the sons of Jacob with their posterity; for this reason, as well as because of the change of Jacob's name by God Himself, the name Israel became their national designation. (References: Israel's sojourn in and departure from Egypt; the settlement in Canaan and the period of the Judges; God's purposes in the establishment of Israel; the Hebrew element in civilization—see The Bible Work, Vols. I-III.) Points of interest: Depressed and discouraged as were the Israelites in Egypt, they yet kept themselves as a community separate from their Egyptian masters, not acknowledging their gods nor coveting their greatness. The choice of Moses, which so impressed the imagination of the Christian apostle (Heb. 11:24 ff.), was, unconsciously but in a very essential way, the choice of the Hebrew people: otherwise they would have disappeared, long before the Exodus, among the multitudes of desert immigrants continually drawn into the insatiable maw of the empire of the Nile.

中以色列：希伯来民族，即犹太民族。作为一个民族或百姓，包括雅各众子及其后裔；正因如此，又因神亲自为雅各改名，『以色列』遂成为他们民族的名号。（参引：以色列在埃及的寄居与出离；迦南的定居与士师时代；神设立以色列的旨意；文明中的希伯来元素——见《The Bible Work》第一至三卷。）要点：以色列人在埃及虽受压制、灰心丧气，却仍守住自己为一个与埃及主人分别的群体——不承认他们的神祇，不艳羡他们的显赫。摩西的抉择曾令基督使徒的想象深受震动（希伯来书 11:24 以下）；那抉择在不自觉之间、却以极本质的方式，也是希伯来民族的抉择：不然，早在出埃及以前，他们就会消失在源源被吸入尼罗河帝国那贪得无厌之巨口的沙漠移民群中了。

ENWe may divide the history of Canaan into three main periods. First, the time of general preparation—from the earliest antiquity up to and including the days of the Hebrew patriarchs—dominated by Babylonian political influence. Next, a period of special preparation: not only was the land of Canaan prepared for Israel, but Israel was prepared for the land of Canaan; this era is marked by controlling Egyptian influence. Finally came the time of the Hebrew occupation, marked by a gradual extension and absorption of the Canaanites, and the conversion of the country into Jehovah's land for Jehovah's people. The publication of the famous El Amarna letters—written to the Egyptian court, three hundred miles up the Nile, by Egyptian officials in Palestine, in the Babylonian language and script—has given us a rare glimpse into the life of Palestine two hundred years before the Exodus. This is the most remarkable literary fact of ancient history, showing that Babylonian civilization, literature and education followed upon the long Babylonian occupation of the West-land, and maintained their ground hundreds of years after the political influence of the empire of the Euphrates had entirely ceased. Babylonia conditioned the beginning of Israel, and the ending of its political existence. In the interval between its occupations of Canaan comes the Egyptian control, which itself, in the strange workings of Providence, was overruled so as to make a way for the people of Israel. Strange, that while Egypt within her own territory was preparing that people for their future career, she should also be preparing a home for them in another land! No wonder it was said, many ages afterward, by the mouth of a Hebrew prophet: "When Israel was a child, then I loved him, and called My son out of Egypt." Politically, Israel was always relatively unimportant among the nations; and the Hebrew tenure of the soil of Palestine was relatively short—of the four thousand years or thereabout before the Christian era through which we can follow the history of Western Asia, Palestine was held independently by the Hebrew race for only one eighth of the time. And yet the possession of this insignificant area by Israel determines ultimately the destiny of the whole wide earth. There was positively no other local habitation possible for the people of Jehovah, where they could work out the providential destiny which was to make them, instrumentally, the saviors of the world. J. F. McCurdy.

F. McCurdy）

麦柯迪史纲一：总论 / McCurdy's Outlines I: General Summary

ENBroad historical outlines of Israel's connection with Babylonia and Assyria, with Egypt and Syria—by Professor J. F. McCurdy (Homiletic Review, 1896). I. General Summary: a few far-reaching conclusions arrived at with the help of the lately exhumed monuments of Oriental antiquity. 1. The Old Testament is a history of the ancient Northern Semites, and among them especially of the people of Israel, to whom and by whom has come the knowledge of God's works and ways. 2. Viewed outwardly, the history of Israel is an episode in the larger history of the states of Western Asia. Israel was itself an offshoot of the race that controlled for thousands of years the whole great region between the Mediterranean and the Tigris; it shared with the kindred peoples some of its fundamental institutions—political, social, and religious; its destiny was determined also by the movements of the larger states dominant within that circle. 3. The great controlling factor among the nations, and the disposer of the destiny of Israel, was the dual empire and civilization of Babylonia and Assyria. With Babylonia the Old Testament history begins, and with it Israel's career as a nation ends. 4. Viewed from within, these relations of Israel were so ordered and disposed that they ministered materially to its higher life. Israel received the word of Jehovah; but only by prolonged and drastic discipline could the moral and spiritual truths of revelation be made real to its recipients. 5. The golden age of prophecy in Israel—the most active period of revelation, the most productive and influential era of Hebrew literature—was coincident with the time of the closest contact between Israel and the Eastern empire. To understand those relations is to hold a key to some of the priceless treasures of the Word of God. 6. The Babylonians and Assyrians were the best record-keepers of the ancient East: their surviving annals are fairly full and on the whole accurate, and serve at once to explain and to supplement the Hebrew records. They were also the best time-keepers of antiquity: their chronological notices furnish a framework for the Old Testament history. 7. Assyriology holds already a foremost place in Biblical apologetics: it furnishes by far the best tests of the historical accuracy of the Old Testament—(a) directly, by the actual record: all the numerous statements as to international events made in the historical books are verified by the cuneiform annals wherever the same topics are touched upon in the two literatures; (b) indirectly, through the larger interpretation of history: the many allusions in the Bible to peoples outside of Israel are shown by the reconstructed history of Western Asia to bear an essential part in the unfolding of the providential purpose displayed in the discipline and education of God's ancient people for the salvation of the race.

中以色列与巴比伦、亚述以及埃及、叙利亚关系的历史大纲——麦柯迪教授撰（《讲道评论》1896 年）。一、总论：借助新近出土的东方古代碑碣得出的几条影响深远的结论。1. 旧约是古代北闪族的历史，其中尤以以色列民为主——认识神的作为与法则的知识，正是临到他们、又借他们传开的。2. 从外部看，以色列的历史是西亚诸邦更大历史中的一段插曲。以色列本身就是那个数千年间控制着地中海与底格里斯河之间整片大区域之种族的支脉；它与诸同宗民族共有若干根本制度——政治的、社会的、宗教的；它的命运也由那圈同宗民族中居主导地位的大国的动向所决定。3. 列国间的最大支配因素、以色列命运的处置者，是巴比伦—亚述这对孪生帝国及其文明。旧约历史以巴比伦开篇，以色列的国族生涯也随巴比伦而落幕。4. 从内部看，以色列的这些国际关系被安排调度得恰恰实质性地服务于它更高的生命。以色列领受了耶和华的话；但唯有借漫长而剧烈的管教，启示中的道德与属灵真理才能对领受者变得真切。5. 以色列预言的黄金时代——启示最活跃的时期、希伯来文学最多产最有影响的年代——恰与以色列同东方帝国接触最密的时期重合。明白那些关系，就握住了打开神话语中若干无价珍藏的一把钥匙。6. 巴比伦人与亚述人是古代东方最好的记录者：他们存世的编年史相当完备、大体准确，恰可解释并补充希伯来的记载。他们还是古代最好的计时者：其纪年提供了旧约历史的年代框架。7. 亚述学在圣经护教学中已居前列：它为旧约的历史准确性提供了迄今最好的检验——（a）直接的，凭实际记录：历史书中关于国际事件的众多陈述，凡两种文献同涉一题之处，无不为楔形文字编年史所证实；（b）间接的，凭对历史的更大解读：圣经中对以色列以外诸民族的大量提及，经重建的西亚史显明，在神管教教育其古代子民以拯救人类这一护理旨意的展开中，占有本质性的一环。

史纲二：存国的国际条件；亚兰插曲 / II: International Conditions; The Aramaean Interlude

ENII. Great points in Israel's history. International conditions which preserved Israelitish nationality: Canaan was not normally a country left to itself, open to chance invaders. On account of its international position and strategic advantages it was greatly coveted by the ruling powers of the ancient East, and usually occupied by one or the other of them. Thus the independent rule of Israel was but a brief interlude in the long and checkered history of the Holy Land—a history of national and racial vicissitude, of dependence and servitude. This somewhat startling fact brings out in strong relief the pre-eminent importance of the Hebrew regime in Palestine—so brief and politically so insignificant, and yet fraught with infinite and world-wide issues. Israel's occupation of the Promised Land was made possible through the retirement of Egypt, after the fierce conflicts with the Hittites had left either power incapable of holding a permanent empire in Western Asia. Both Hittites and Egyptians, however, followed a more ancient and powerful claimant: they were, so to speak, the residuary legatees of the Babylonians. For many centuries before the Egyptians ventured to set foot in Palestine, the Babylonians had colonized, ruled, and civilized the whole Mediterranean coastland. When either Babylonia or Assyria was unquestionably supreme in the East, the control of the West-land fell to it almost as a matter of course. But for centuries before and after the entrance of Israel into Canaan, Babylonia and Assyria were contending with one another as rivals upon nearly equal terms: this was accordingly the era of the conflicts of Egyptians and Hittites for the possession of the West, and of the appearance of Israel as one among the nations. This period of strife between the empires of the Euphrates and the Tigris involved also the era of Israel's growth as a people, of the rise of the monarchy, of the political, moral, and religious changes which conditioned the reception and progress of Revelation. Thus it came to pass that when Assyria reached undisputed predominance, the fate of Syria and Palestine was sealed, and one of the kingdoms of Israel was obliterated; and when Assyria in its turn gave way to the reviving Chaldean power, the other kingdom of Israel came to an end. Such are, in broadest outline, the conditions which made it possible for Israel to secure and retain a refuge and a home for itself and its religion in the intervals between larger international movements.

中二、以色列历史的大关节。保全以色列国族的国际条件：迦南在常态下并不是一块无主之地、任凭偶来的入侵者染指。因其国际位置与战略优势，它为古代东方的列强所艳羡，通常总被其中一强占据。因此，以色列的独立统治不过是圣地漫长而斑驳历史中的短暂插曲——那是一部民族与种族兴替、依附与奴役的历史。这个多少令人吃惊的事实，反而把希伯来政权在巴勒斯坦的超凡重要性衬托得分外鲜明——如此短促、政治上如此无足轻重，却孕育着无穷而及于全世界的后果。以色列得以占据应许之地，是因埃及的引退——与赫人的恶战使两强都无力在西亚维持长久的帝国。然而赫人与埃及人都跟在一个更古老更强大的先主之后：可以说，他们是巴比伦人的剩余遗产继承人。早在埃及人敢于踏足巴勒斯坦之前许多世纪，巴比伦人已在整个地中海沿岸殖民、统治、施化。每当巴比伦或亚述在东方独大无疑，西土的控制权几乎顺理成章归它。但在以色列进迦南前后的数百年间，巴比伦与亚述势均力敌、互为劲敌：这便是埃及人与赫人争夺西方、以色列借着定居巴勒斯坦而跻身列国的年代。幼发拉底与底格里斯两帝国相争的这一时期，也正是以色列作为民族成长、王国兴起、政治道德宗教诸变迁制约着启示之领受与进展的年代。于是，当亚述取得无可争议的优势，叙利亚与巴勒斯坦的命运便告注定，以色列两国之一（北国）被抹去；及至亚述又让位于复兴的迦勒底势力，以色列的另一国（犹大）也走到尽头。以上就是最粗线条的轮廓：正是这些条件，使以色列得以在更大国际运动的间隙里，为自己和自己的宗教夺得并保有一处避难所与家园。

ENIsrael's connections with Syria: Of Israel's closer rivals, the most influential were the Aramaeans ("Syrians") of Damascus. We meet here a very remarkable phenomenon: the degree of political influence exerted by the kindred peoples upon the Hebrews was in inverse ratio to their geographical nearness, and even to their family relationship. The nearest kindred of Israel—the Edomites, Moabites, and Ammonites—were often its fierce rivals; their function, however, was rather to hold in check and to chasten the people of Jehovah than permanently to affect their career. But the Aramaeans had a task to fulfill toward Israel which has given them their chief historical importance, and a conspicuous place in the history of Revelation: their relations with Israel largely determined the rise and development of a cycle of Old Testament prophecy. The Aramaeans came in force over the Euphrates after the wars between the Egyptians and Hittites. The Hebrews had scarcely been settled in Canaan before they had to undergo a term of subjection to Cushan-rishathaim, king of Mesopotamia. In the time of David we read of several powerful Aramaean communities reaching from the Euphrates to Damascus: this greatest and most enterprising king of Israel put them under tribute, and thus fixed the traditional and ideal limits of the "kingdom of David" for all coming time. But before the reign of Solomon was over, all trace of Aramaean subjection to Israel was gone. Half a century after the division of the kingdom, Asa of Judah even called in the aid of Benhadad I of Damascus against his rival, Baasha of Northern Israel—with the result that valuable Galilean territory was incorporated into the rising kingdom of Damascus. Henceforth, until the Assyrian came upon the West-land, the Aramaeans held the leading position; and such predominance would in all human probability have proved fatal to both kingdoms of Israel, had it not been put an end to by the intervention of the Assyrians.

中以色列与叙利亚的关系：在以色列较近的对手中，影响最大的是大马士革的亚兰人（即圣经的『叙利亚人』）。我们在此遇见一个极堪注意的现象：同宗诸族对希伯来人施加的政治影响，与其地理的邻近程度、甚至与其血缘的亲疏，恰成反比。以色列血缘最近的亲族——以东人、摩押人、亚扪人——固然常是它凶悍的对头；但他们的功能，与其说是长久左右耶和华子民的前程，不如说是牵制并管教他们。亚兰人对以色列却负有一项使命——这使命给了他们主要的历史重要性，也给了他们在启示史中的显要位置：他们与以色列的关系，很大程度上决定了旧约一个预言系列的兴起与展开。埃及人与赫人的战争之后，亚兰人大举渡过幼发拉底。希伯来人在迦南立足未稳，就先受了米所波大米王古珊利萨田一个时期的辖制。到大卫的时代，我们读到从幼发拉底直到大马士革之间有数个强盛的亚兰邦：以色列这位最伟大、最有进取心的君王使它们纳贡称臣，从而为万代立定了『大卫之国』传统而理想的疆界。但所罗门在位未终，亚兰臣服以色列的痕迹已荡然无存。王国分裂半个世纪后，犹大的亚撒竟招引大马士革的便哈达一世来对付他的对头、北国的巴沙——结果加利利的宝贵疆土并入了方兴的大马士革王国。从此直到亚述人临到西土，亚兰人占据首位；这种优势，若非亚述人的干预将其终结，按人之常情推断，早晚必置以色列两国于死地。

ENObserve the principal stages in the providential process. Northern Israel was first made a strong, self-contained state by the genius of Omri, the founder of Samaria; it was he and his dynasty who maintained the independence of Israel against the Aramaeans. Three notable features mark the era of the house of Omri: the promotion of the Phoenician Baal-worship; the Syrian wars; and the ministry of the great political prophets, Elijah and Elisha—this last provoked and largely determined by the other two, and marking the beginning of that wide and keen interest in the external relations of Israel which is so essential an element in Hebrew prophecy. In the midst of the wars between Benhadad II of Damascus and Ahab of Israel, news suddenly came (854 B.C.) that Shalmaneser II of Assyria was marching down from the Euphrates. At once the kingdoms of the West-land laid aside their habitual strife and combined to meet the invader: foremost in the array stood the Aramaeans of Damascus; side by side with them—for the first and only time—stood the chariots and foot-soldiers of Israel. The confederacy was defeated; but it showed the intruders what the obstacles were to their westward march. The most critical period for Israel came with the fall of the house of Omri: the dynasty of Jehu was unable to withstand the onslaughts of the terrible regicide Hazael, under whom Damascus rose to a height of military power never equaled by any native community of Syria or Palestine. Northern Israel was almost obliterated, and the kingdom of Judah brought to terms of submission. How then was Israel rescued? The answers till lately lay under the earth. Now we learn that it was during a temporary withdrawal of the Assyrians that Hazael had scope for action in Palestine; a few years thereafter the Assyrians returned, and under Ramman-nirari III the city of Damascus itself was at length taken (797 B.C.), and thenceforward never took a leading place among the nations. This was the opportunity of both Israel and Judah—for the Assyrians themselves then remained inactive for nearly half a century. Thus we account for the prosperous reigns of Jeroboam II and Uzziah. One thing more we learn, of the very highest significance: at this period, when Israel was reaping the bitter fruits of its prosperity as well as of its adversity, Amos and Hosea came forward as the first of the great literary prophets. With them the high career of Damascus is a thing of the past; they look forward, with marvelous political and moral insight and foresight, to the return of the Assyrian hosts and the captivity of faithless, dissolute Israel. How different would have been the history of Israel and Judah—how different the course of Revelation itself—if Assyria had not done its brief but effective work upon Damascus!

史纲三：亚述与北国的被掳 / III: Assyria and Israel's First Captivity

ENAssyria and Israel's first captivity. Next to the Assyrians and Babylonians themselves, the Aramaeans of Damascus were the most dangerous and persistent of all the foes of ancient Israel—for many years more than a mere rival, its superior in power, and seemingly its destined conqueror. Only one chance remained for Israel: that a nation greater still should hold these enemies in check. This was accomplished by the Assyrians. In their persistent attempts to subjugate the West, the Syrians of Damascus were almost the only people of consequence to oppose them; they waged an unequal contest, and the result determined the survival of Israel until the completion of its work for the world's religion. Soon there comes another period—the most momentous of all for the national and religious life of Israel: the aggressor now is the Assyrian himself. 2 Kings 15:29 tells of the first systematic invasion of Israelitish territory by the soldiers of Asshur. Northern Israel, in the time of its latest kings, fell a prey to moral and religious corruption and internal discord; in the reign of the usurping king Pekah it formed an unprecedented and anomalous combination with Damascus against the kingdom of Judah, then under the weak and idolatrous Ahaz; and Ahaz called in the help of the king of Assyria, with the result so disastrous to the northern kingdom. "Tiglath-Pileser . . . carried away captive": these words, looked at in the light now thrown upon them by the cuneiform inscriptions, stand for the most far-reaching political movement of the whole history of the ancient East. Tiglath-Pileser III was one of the few great leaders of men who have turned the course of the world's history: it was he who determined that there was to be an empire in Western Asia, instead of a multitude of petty states in perpetual conflict; by renewing Assyrian predominance he set the example of imperial rule which has since been followed by the founders of all the great empires of the Western world. (In v. 39 of the same chapter the Assyrian invader who had been bought off by Menahem is twice called "Pul"; all difficulty has been removed by evidence showing that Pul, or Pulu, was the original name of the great ruler, who assumed the other name in imitation of Tiglath-Pileser I. Thus the accuracy of the Biblical narrative is surprisingly attested.)

ENBut of far more significance than his achievements in war was the policy of which Tiglath-Pileser was the chief promoter: the method of dealing with subject peoples which he brought to a system. He made it his business to carry away to distant regions the inhabitants of countries which offered stubborn resistance. The device was not new; but hitherto it had not been employed as a matter of fundamental state policy. Our text gives the first instance of its application to the land of Israel. Henceforth a new factor was added to the political, social, and religious forces of Israel's national life; a new dread entered into the forebodings of the patriot, and a new prevision into the outlook of the prophet. As the great subverter of the nations went on irresistibly "removing the boundaries of the peoples" (Isa. 10:13), the thought of deportation and exile became ever more familiar to serious minds in Israel, and colored and molded all the subsequent literature. In the annals of the later Assyrian kings, the fate of Israel—its gradual dismemberment, its removal and dispersion—appears as but a commonplace instance of the working of a gigantic system; but these incidents are lifted out of historical commonplace by the universal significance of Israel's special struggle, its far-reaching, or rather its eternal, spiritual issues. How the prophets of Israel interwove them into the fabric of their discourse, no open-eyed reader can fail to observe. And while the annals of Tiglath-Pileser make mention of the very transactions recorded in our text, the whole movement was in the mind of a keen and watchful prophet of Israel more than twenty years before: "Israel shall surely be led away captive out of his land" (Amos 7:17); "therefore will I cause you to go into captivity beyond Damascus, saith Jehovah" (Amos 5:27).

史纲四：亚述与希西家 / IV: Assyria and Hezekiah, King of Judah

ENAssyria and Hezekiah, king of Judah. Why does the Old Testament, as contrasted with the New, concern itself with secular history and with the politics even of Gentile nations? Why do the Benhadads, and Shalmaneser, and Sennacherib, and the Rabshakeh find a place in the sacred records along with Moses and Samuel and David and Isaiah? The reason is mainly this: that the progress of the kingdom of God among men depended essentially upon the preservation of the people of God as a nation. During the period of Old Testament revelation, Israel was working out, under its peculiar political and social system, the truth of God as it was being "spoken unto the fathers in the prophets by divers portions and in divers manners." The destruction of the Jewish state would annul these outward conditions. At the coming of the Christ, the Jewish Church had long been the depository, under the guardianship of powerful empires, of a revelation complete in itself; and when the new and fuller doctrine came to be given, the world had been providentially prepared for its wider propagation: the prophet was now replaced by the missionary. The true religion, being no longer confined to a single state, needed no longer a national arena or continuous political protection. The Northern Kingdom had fallen, never to be restored; the future of the kingdom of God was now committed to the smaller and weaker of the two kingdoms. Isaiah and Micah had watched with intensest interest the decline and fall of Samaria, and put on record the moral of that catastrophe. There rested ever upon the faithful the one overshadowing dread: that Assyria might complete the obliteration of Israel by the ruin of Judah and Jerusalem, and thus make an end of the worship of Jehovah and of His earthly dwelling-place.

ENHow did the chances stand for the survival of Judah and Jerusalem? Ahaz had become a client of the great Tiglath-Pileser, and had introduced into the worship of Jehovah some of the more imposing features of Assyrian worship. Almost coincidently with the fall of Samaria occurred his death and the accession of young Hezekiah. In religious matters the prophets could direct the new ruler, thanks to the tutelage of the watchful Isaiah; but in political affairs Hezekiah was, for at least half of his reign, under the influence of a restless, intriguing war-party in Jerusalem, whose chief ally was the ambitious but unreliable empire of the Nile. During the former half of his reign the redoubtable Sargon controlled the affairs of Western Asia; the Assyrian monarch speaks of himself in one surviving document as the devastator of the land of Judah. When light falls again from the monuments, we find Jerusalem itself threatened with destruction, because it was in direct rebellion as a member of a league of disaffected states. There is no stage of Israel's history more satisfactorily illustrated by the cuneiform records than this most critical era: Jerusalem, Lachish; Hezekiah, Sennacherib, even the remote Merodach-baladan of Babylon—the parts played by all of these are set forth in the inscribed or sculptured memorials of Assyria. Sennacherib came to the throne in 705 B.C. Merodach-baladan, the usurping king of Babylon, an adventurous and heroic Chaldean, lost no time in advancing his aims against the Assyrian oppressor: he sent an embassy to Hezekiah with a letter and presents—the ostensible object, to congratulate him upon his recovery from sickness; the real purpose, to engage him in revolt. Hezekiah actively interfered among the Philistine cities to further the conspiracy, promoting a revolution in Ekron by seizing its kinglet, who was loyal to Sennacherib. Thus the policy of Judah had brought the kingdom, and with it the religion of Jehovah, to the extremest point of peril: it was a revolt stirred up from the Nile to the Tigris, and in it Hezekiah was an arch-mover and Jerusalem a center. If Assyria should be victorious—and Isaiah did not fail to proclaim aloud (chap. 10) the irresistible might of the Great King—the fate of Judah and Jerusalem must be exemplary and terrible.

ENSennacherib now proceeds to take his revenge. In the spring of 701 he marches with a great army down the western coastland; secures the allegiance of southern Phoenicia; then divides his forces—one part dealing with the revolted Philistine cities, the other advancing through the heart of Hezekiah's kingdom, taking one city after another by assault, devastating wherever they moved. One glimpse of their march is given us by Isaiah, as he describes in graphic and unusually excited language (chap. 10:28-32) the progress of the relentless enemy through the pass of Michmash to the very environs of Jerusalem. Our text (2 Kings 18:13) is the only reference in the historical books to this awful campaign of carnage, desolation, and distress; now we may compare the Assyrian account, given in the name of Sennacherib himself: "Hezekiah the Judaite, who had not submitted to my yoke—forty-six of his fenced cities, and fortresses, and small towns in their vicinity without number, by battering-rams and the assaults of the breach-stormers and the strokes of axes, I besieged and took; 200,150 persons, small and great, male and female, horses, mules, asses, camels, large cattle and small cattle without number, I brought forth from the midst of them and allotted as spoil." Much more do the monuments record: the surrender by the overawed Hezekiah of the captive king of Ekron; the blockade of Jerusalem; the submission of Hezekiah; his payment of tribute; the inroad of the Egyptian army of relief and its crushing defeat; the capture of Ekron, Askalon, and Lachish—the last set forth in pictorial sculpture. No less instructive are the significant omissions of the official Assyrian report: the failure to tell of the second investment of Jerusalem and its sudden termination; of the plague-stroke in the marshes of Pelusium; and of the ignominious retreat from Palestine. We apprehend now, as never in any intervening age, the chances of Judah's destruction—from the resentment of the invincible king against an obstinate rebel, from the ruin and desolation of the territory, from the utter helplessness of the isolated capital and of the impoverished ruler who sat upon the tottering throne of David. And above and controlling all is revealed, closer and stronger than ever before, the arm of Jehovah, stretched out to smite and to save. J. F. McCurdy.
